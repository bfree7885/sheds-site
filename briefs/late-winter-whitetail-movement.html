Late Winter Whitetail Movement: What GPS Studies Show

Published March 4, 2026

Long-term GPS collar studies of white-tailed deer show that habitat use and movement patterns shift significantly by season. Late winter is not simply a quieter version of fall — it is a distinct behavioral period shaped by energy balance, landscape structure, and environmental stress.

Seasonal Space Use Contracts

Multi-year tracking research shows that deer often reduce the size of their active home ranges during late winter. Movement becomes more conservative, centered around reliable food sources and protective cover. Energy conservation becomes a priority as body reserves decline following the rut and as natural forage quality remains limited.

Habitat Selection Shifts

In colder months, deer show increased selection for thermal cover — including conifer stands, dense edge habitat, and areas buffered from prevailing wind. Open travel decreases during extreme weather events. Movement corridors become more predictable but more tightly constrained to cover and food proximity.

Weather Amplifies Patterns

Snow depth and prolonged cold intensify this contraction effect. Studies consistently show that as energetic costs increase, deer prioritize minimized travel distance between bedding and feeding areas. Short, repeatable routes become more common than exploratory movement.

Why This Matters

Late winter behavior is not random. It reflects biological restraint. Understanding this seasonal contraction of space use helps frame how and where deer are likely to concentrate activity before spring green-up begins.
