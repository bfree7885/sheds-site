Sheds Field Guide

The Field Guide explains the basic patterns behind whitetail behavior and shed hunting. Unlike Field Notes, these topics are evergreen and rarely change.

Whitetail Biology

Why Deer Shed Antlers

Whitetail deer shed their antlers every winter as part of their natural annual cycle. After the breeding season, testosterone levels drop quickly. This hormonal change weakens the connection between the antler and the pedicle — the bony base where antlers grow.

As winter progresses, the connection breaks down and the antlers eventually fall off. This usually happens between January and early March depending on region, age, and winter conditions.

Older bucks often shed earlier because the stress of the rut leaves them with lower energy reserves. Younger deer and healthier animals sometimes hold antlers longer.

Basic Deer Habitat

Whitetail deer need three main habitat elements to survive: food, cover, and security. Good deer habitat provides all three within a relatively small area.

Food sources may include agricultural crops, mast such as acorns, natural browse, and winter woody plants. Cover includes thick vegetation, young forest growth, or conifer stands that protect deer from wind and weather.

Security comes from areas where deer feel safe from disturbance. These locations are often thick bedding areas, steep terrain, or locations away from regular human activity.

When these elements occur close together, deer movement becomes predictable. This is one reason terrain features such as ridges, edges, and transition zones are important for understanding deer behavior.

Shed Hunting Basics

Shed hunting is the practice of searching for antlers that deer naturally drop during late winter. Success depends on understanding where deer spend time during this part of the year.

During late winter, deer move less and focus on conserving energy. They often concentrate in areas that offer thermal protection, reliable food, and minimal disturbance.

Because of this behavior, the most productive shed hunting areas are often close to bedding cover, winter food sources, and travel corridors between the two.

Ethical shed hunting also means recognizing when deer are under heavy stress from winter conditions. If animals appear extremely thin or easily disturbed, it is often better to wait until conditions improve.
